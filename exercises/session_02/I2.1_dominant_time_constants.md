# I2.1 — Dominant time constants of M02 (independent)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Learning objective** | Relate the eigenvalues of a state-space model to the time scales visible in its response (session outcome 2; reused in Sessions 6 and 8). |
| **Prerequisites** | Lesson §4 (M02 state-space form); Session 1 (time constant of M01). |
| **Starting files** | `exercises/session_02/s02_i1_rc.py` |
| **Expected duration** | 10 min |
| **Expected outcome** | Two time constants (well under 1 h and close to a day) computed from the eigenvalues; for each mode, which temperatures move; why the 63 % rise of $T_z$ comes earlier than the slow time constant. |

Use the default M02 parameters (`ZoneRCParams()`: estimates for a 120 m² house from Bacher &
Madsen 2011), $T_{amb}$ = 0 °C.

## Instructions

1. Build $A$ with `energy_training.simulation.zone_rc.state_space` and compute its eigenvalues and
   eigenvectors (`numpy.linalg.eig`). Convert the eigenvalues to time constants in hours.
2. For each mode, look at the eigenvector: do $T_z$ and $T_e$ move together or against each other?
   Which physical process does each mode describe?
3. Start from equilibrium with the heater at 1 kW (`steady_state`) and step the heater to 4 kW.
   Simulate 96 h. When has $T_z$ completed 63.2 % of its total rise? Compare with the slow time
   constant and explain the difference.

## Extension activity

Reduce M02 to one state in two ways: (a) M01 with the same total capacity and conductance
(`lumped_equivalent`); (b) neglect $C_z$ so that the zone follows the envelope instantly. Which
reduction reproduces $T_z$ better over 96 h, and which time constant does each one have?
